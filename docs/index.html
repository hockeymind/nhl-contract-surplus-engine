NHL Contract Surplus Engine

What a contract is worth in goals and dollars, how well that forecast holds up, and where it breaks.

This model estimates how much value an NHL skater delivers over the life of a contract, prices that value in cap dollars, and compares it to the cap hit. The difference is surplus. The goal is a tool a front office could use to ask, before signing, what the next five or eight years cost if the player ages normally and the cap grows.

How it works

1. Value. Each skater-season is scored in goals above replacement (GAR) from six pieces: even-strength offence and defence, power-play offence, penalty-kill defence, penalties drawn minus taken, and finishing. Rates are measured against the player's own team and shrunk toward the position mean. Replacement level is forward 13+ and defenseman 7+ by team ice time. WAR is GAR divided by 6.

2. Forecast. Next-season value is a weighted blend of a 82-game projection, the last two seasons, and a regression term for how good the player already was. It is fit on 1,126 contracts with realized seasons, with seasons put on one scale across eras.

f = −1.93 + 0.903·proj82 + 0.407·g1 + 0.085·g2 − 0.778·hist1

3. Aging. Later years follow the age curve scaled by 0.53 (SE 0.10, 1,440 player-years). That scale is fit on the same data the final model uses, so it is not an out-of-sample number.

4. Price. Cap share is modeled from forecast, age, free-agent status and term, separately for forwards and defensemen, on all 1,515 signings. Dollars per goal come from the market itself rather than a fixed number.

5. Cap path. Actual cap through 2026-27 ($104M), then 2%, 4% and 6% growth. Those growth rates are an assumption, and they matter a lot for long deals.

Does the forecast work?

The test that counts: train on contracts signed 2018–22, with outcomes cut off at 2022, and score 350 deals signed 2023–24 against what happened through 2025. The age tables were rebuilt using only information available at each contract's signing season.

What this does and doesn't show

  • The forecast beats guessing the average by a wide margin. It beats "just use last season" by 0.12 GAR/82. That is real but small, and I would not oversell it.
  • The market adjustment (letting a player's salary signal what teams know that stats don't) improves the error to 6.74.
  • Some small leaks remain: shrinkage constants and replacement level use all seasons. The aging scale and the final weights are fit on 2018–24, so published parameters are not out of sample.

Uncertainty

Single-season results are noisy, so the model gives a range. The interval widths were fit on 2007–16 and checked on 2017 onward. The target is 80% coverage.

Worked example: an eight-year deal with a flat cap hit

Jesper Bratt's 8-year, $7.875M deal is a good stress test because the cap hit never moves while the cap does. At 4% cap growth, the model puts total surplus at about $9.1M ($7.3M at 2% growth, $10.9M at 6%), with an 80% band of roughly ±$28M. With the cap frozen the surplus is a loss of about $4.6M. Almost the entire gap comes from the assumption about the cap, not about the player.

Where it breaks

  • Defensemen are provisional. GAR undercounts what defensemen do. Treat every defense number as a rough read.
  • Big forwards are probably undervalued. Forwards at 7%+ of the cap beat forecast by about 1.0 GAR/82 (SE 0.6, n=109), but the gap swings by period (−0.8 for 2018–20, about +2.3 since 2021). I apply a correction, and it is probably still too small for recent deals.
  • Price drift. The 2026 top end is underpredicted by about 2 points of cap share. The model assumes 30–37% of a price premium comes back as performance, and that cannot yet be validated on 2026 signings.
  • Age 34+ estimates are unreliable. No goalies.
  • Recent deals have little evidence. Contracts from 2025 on have at most one realized season.
  • Cap hit is not salary paid, entry-level deals and sub-$1M contracts are excluded, and the penalty term is a simple net of drawn minus taken.
